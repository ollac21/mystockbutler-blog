## What does a report add to a fair-value number?

A score answers the question you thought to ask. A report answers the ones you didn't. Here is the difference on a real report, [Alphabet's](/stocks/googl/).

### "What is it worth, and how sure are you?"

The report gives three cases, with probabilities: bear 25%, base 55%, bull 20%. By October 2029 the shares are worth $173.78, $361.67 or $637.35 in those cases. The weighted price is $369.83, a 15% return including dividends, against $324.89 on 28 October 2026. The bear case needs AI answers to cut paid clicks while ad-tech remedies and heavy spending weaken cash returns. The bull case needs Cloud backlog to turn into profit. You see the range, and what moves it. No buy or sell call.

### "How much cash does it really make?"

Alphabet's free cash flow in 2025 was $73,266 million. The report takes off $24,953 million of stock pay, the shares given to employees, and gets to $48,313 million of real free cash flow. We count it that way for every company.

### "Where do the numbers come from?"

Every number traced to its source, with 321 sources cited in the Alphabet report. Click, read the filing, decide.

### "Who runs the company, and what have they said?"

Beside each report is a research hub: management bios, interview links, the last 8 earnings-call transcripts and a table of insider filings.

### Is one fair-value number enough to decide?

Rarely. One number hides the range around it. A report shows three cases with probabilities, so you can see what has to go right and what happens if it doesn't.

For a terminal with charts and transcripts, Koyfin. For global financials and a valuation model, TIKR. For screens and portfolios, Stock Rover. Every number is traced to its source and every source is listed. A final check corrects or deletes statements it finds untrue. AI models write the reports, so one can still get a number wrong: check the numbers you rely on.
